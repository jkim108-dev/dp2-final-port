Bio Acoustic Panel

A collaborative acoustic panel project made entirely from biomaterials, exploring cork, wood sawdust, coconut fiber, and cotton through material experiments, layered prototypes, and reflected-sound comparisons.

Material & construction

The project explores several constructions, including cork-faced panels with wood-sawdust cores and layered samples incorporating coconut fiber and cotton. Binder proportions and drying conditions were adjusted across experiments.

Binder system: water, glycerin, starch, and gelatin. Recipes varied between samples.

TEAM PROJECT · DAVID / JUHYOUNG / JAY

From material experiments to physical panels

Our team explored cork, wood sawdust, coconut fiber, and cotton as alternatives to petroleum-based acoustic panel materials. More than 40 experiments tested binder proportions, drying conditions, porous textures, and layered construction.

01 / Make, dry, compare

Early trials tested starch- and gelatin-based binders with water and glycerin. The experiment log records brittle surfaces, weak bonding, warping, and mold during drying. Later trials explored controlled dehydrator drying and layered structures while trying to keep the material porous.

02 / Four physical iterations

The prototypes show the progression from a single-material sample to layered panels and a molded cork surface. The log notes that adding more layers introduced challenges with liquid absorption, bonding, and warping.

03 / Build a repeatable sound comparison

We built an MDF enclosure with a speaker and recorder to compare reflected sound. Panel size and equipment positions were kept consistent. Recordings at 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 3000, and 4000 Hz were compared with a no-panel baseline.

04 / Compare frequency response

The final comparison shows that response varied by frequency: several samples recorded lower levels than the baseline around 500 Hz, while results at higher frequencies were mixed. This supported exploring combinations of modules rather than treating one composition as equally effective across every frequency.

These are relative recording-level comparisons in the team's test enclosure, not standardized sound-absorption coefficients or certified room-acoustic ratings. Negative values in the change graph indicate a lower recorded level than the no-panel baseline.

05 / Present the material system

The final critique brought the physical samples, material documentation, and testing apparatus together to explain how the panels developed through making and comparison.
